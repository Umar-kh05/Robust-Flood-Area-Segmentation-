Abstract—Accurate floodwater segmentation supports emer-
gency response, exposure assessment, and recovery planning, yet
robust models require geographically diverse labeled imagery that
is often distributed across separate data owners. Federated learn-
ing (FL) permits collaborative model training while raw images
remain at their source, but strong cross-dataset heterogeneity
can cause unstable optimization and client drift. This review
analyzes 20 studies spanning federated optimization, federated
semantic segmentation, and remote-sensing flood mapping. It
motivates a controlled comparison of FedAvg, FedProx, FedNova,
and SCAFFOLD using one independently collected Sentinel-1
flood dataset per client: ETCI 2021, MMFlood, and Kuro Siwo.
A common U-Net and harmonized binary target will isolate the
effect of the FL optimizer. The reviewed evidence shows that
advanced algorithms address different types of heterogeneity
and that no method is uniformly superior. It also shows that
flood studies remain primarily centralized or single-dataset. The
resulting gap is the absence of a controlled comparison of
established FL optimizers for temporary-floodwater segmentation
under real cross-dataset client heterogeneity.
Keywords—federated learning, floodwater segmentation, non-
IID data, Sentinel-1, U-Net, remote sensing
I. I NTRODUCTION
Floods are among the most destructive natural hazards,
causing loss of life, displacement, infrastructure damage, and
long-term economic disruption. Rapid delineation of inundated
areas is therefore important for emergency response, resource
allocation, exposure assessment, and post-disaster recovery.
Earth-observation systems can survey large or inaccessible
areas, while Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR), particularly
Sentinel-1, remains useful during flood events because it
operates day and night and is less obstructed by clouds than
optical sensing. Flood mapping is nevertheless not identical to
general water detection. Permanent rivers, lakes, and reservoirs
may already be present before an event; operational mapping
must identify temporary inundation without treating all surface
water as newly flooded [14], [17].
Semantic segmentation provides a pixel-level solution to
this problem. Encoder–decoder networks such as U-Net learn
both contextual and spatial information and have produced
useful flood masks from Sentinel-1 imagery [13], [18]. Their
reliability, however, depends on representative annotations
across events, climates, land covers, and acquisition conditions.
Public flood datasets differ in geographic coverage, sensor
preprocessing, label generation, class balance, temporal con-
text, and sample count [16], [17], [20]. A model trained on
one source can consequently perform poorly when deployed
on another domain; recent cross-benchmark evidence confirms
that individual-dataset models frequently lose accuracy outside
their training distribution [19].
Centralized multi-dataset training can increase diversity,
but it assumes that every institution can transfer imagery and
annotations to one location. Storage, bandwidth, ownership,
governance, and geospatial-security constraints may make that
assumption impractical. FL instead sends a shared model to
data-holding clients, performs local optimization, and aggre-
gates model updates at a server. Raw imagery remains local,
although FL alone is not a formal privacy guarantee because
protection against information leakage requires mechanisms
such as secure aggregation or differential privacy.
The standard FedAvg algorithm aggregates client parame-
ters in proportion to local sample counts [2]. Its simplicity and
communication efficiency make it the principal baseline, but
several independent flood datasets create more than an artificial
label imbalance. Each client may have a distinct feature
distribution, geography, annotation process, class ratio, and
dataset size. Local updates can therefore move in conflicting
directions. FedProx constrains local deviation from the global
model [3]; SCAFFOLD uses control variates to correct client
drift [4]; and FedNova normalizes updates when clients per-
form unequal amounts of local computation [5]. NIID-Bench
demonstrates that their rankings change across heterogeneity
settings, so superiority cannot be assumed without task-specific
evaluation [6].
The base study by Islam applied a U-Net and FedAvg
to 290 grayscale aerial flood images divided among three
simulated clients [1]. Its federated model achieved 0.7186 IoU
and 0.8363 F1, compared with 0.7450 IoU and 0.8539 F1
under centralized training; federated recall was higher (0.8883
versus 0.8638) but precision was lower (0.7900 versus 0.8443).
These results establish feasibility, yet all clients were partitions
of one Kaggle dataset. Their non-IID behavior came from

II. L ITERATURE REVIEW
This narrative review covers 20 studies selected for direct
relevance to at least one of three components: heterogeneity-
aware FL optimization, federated semantic segmentation, or
flood segmentation and dataset generalization. Sixteen were
published between 2021 and 2025. Four older works are
retained because they introduce the four algorithms being
compared. Tables I–IV report each study’s method, dataset,
evaluation measures, representative performance, and principal
limitation. Scores are reproduced as reported and are not
directly rankable because tasks, splits, architectures, and label
definitions differ.
A. Federated Optimization Under Heterogeneity
FedAvg established iterative local training followed by
weighted parameter averaging [2]. Its communication savings
are substantial, but additional local epochs can amplify drift
when client objectives differ. FedProx adds a proximal penalty
around the current global parameters and is therefore relevant
when independent flood clients produce divergent updates [3].
Its behavior depends on the coefficient µ: weak regularization
resembles FedAvg, while excessive regularization suppresses
useful local learning.
SCAFFOLD estimates server and client control variates
to correct local directions [4]. Its strong results under severe
label heterogeneity make it theoretically attractive for cross-
dataset clients, although maintaining control states increases
implementation and communication cost. FedNova addresses
objective inconsistency by normalizing updates for unequal
local work [5]. Consequently, its central advantage is not fully
tested if all clients always execute identical local steps. A
sound comparison should use equal computation for the main
statistical-heterogeneity experiment and, if resources permit,
add an unequal-local-step stress test.
NIID-Bench is especially important because it compares all
four methods across several non-IID constructions and finds
no universal winner [6]. However, these are mostly artificial
partitions of classification datasets. Islam’s flood experiment
is closer to the target application, but its clients still originate
from one aerial dataset [1]. Table I shows that optimization
evidence is strong, whereas direct evidence for independent
flood domains remains absent.
B. Federated Segmentation and Domain Shift
Federated semantic segmentation adds pixel imbalance
and spatial consistency to the optimization problem. FedDG
uses episodic learning and frequency-space interpolation to
transfer distributional information among medical clients and
improve unseen-domain performance [7]. FedDrive studies
autonomous-driving segmentation under statistical and appear-
ance heterogeneity; its results show that naive FedAvg can
degrade sharply and that local normalization and style aug-
mentation can recover performance [8]. These studies support
evaluation on independent domains, but medical and RGB
driving styles differ physically from SAR backscatter.
FedSeg addresses class-heterogeneous segmentation
through a background-aware loss and pixel-level contrastive
learning [9]. FedST uses diffusion-based style transfer
to synthesize cross-domain appearances [10]. Their gains
demonstrate that segmentation-specific interventions can
exceed optimizer-only baselines, but they alter losses,
representations, or data distributions. They are therefore
related work rather than fair replacements for the proposed
controlled optimizer comparison.
GeoFed is the closest remote-sensing precedent. It models
coupled class-distribution and object-appearance heterogeneity
across institutions and improves land-cover/building segmen-
tation over FedAvg [11]. FedSaaS similarly aligns local and
global class representations with prototypes, contrastive losses,
and adversarial harmonization [12]. Both improve accuracy
but introduce several components and, in FedSaaS, exemplar
transfer. The proposed project deliberately keeps U-Net and
the loss fixed to measure the behavior of four established
optimizers before adding segmentation-specific mechanisms.

D. Flood Datasets and Domain Compatibility
Dataset choice determines whether client heterogeneity is
scientifically meaningful or merely incompatible. FloodNet
supplies rich UA V imagery and multiple damage classes, but
its RGB aerial modality and post-Hurricane-Harvey setting do
not match a common Sentinel-1 VV/VH model input [15]. It is
valuable literature evidence, not an appropriate client for the
final homogeneous-channel experiment. DeepFlood similarly
adds high-resolution aerial and SAR data plus inundated-
vegetation labels, but its four-class regional design and mixed
resolutions would introduce additional experimental factors
[20].
MMFlood and Kuro Siwo are better aligned with satellite
flood delineation. MMFlood covers 95 events in 42 countries
and links Sentinel-1 acquisitions with CEMS flood maps and
auxiliary terrain/hydrography data [16]. Kuro Siwo covers 43
events and explicitly represents floodwater, permanent water,
and non-water using multi-temporal dual-polarization SAR
[17]. Together with ETCI, these datasets provide independent
acquisition and annotation pipelines while retaining a common
Sentinel-1 representation. Their compatibility is not automatic:
masks, no-data conventions, spatial resolution, normalization,
and event-level splits must be audited before training.
E. Critical Synthesis, Research Gap, and Problem Statement
The reviewed literature exposes three recurring trade-offs.
First, optimization methods target different failure modes. Fed-
Prox controls local deviation, SCAFFOLD corrects drift direc-
tion, and FedNova corrects unequal local computation. Their

three bodies of work. Flood segmentation is mainly central-

through conventional FedAvg may converge poorly or favor

annotation heterogeneity. It is unknown whether proximal
regularization, normalized aggregation, or control-variate cor-
rection provides a more accurate, stable, and balanced global
floodwater model under this realistic cross-dataset setting.
The study is guided by four research questions:
1) How do the four algorithms compare in IoU, Dice/F1,
precision, and recall under independent dataset do-
mains?
2) Which method converges most stably and reaches
useful validation performance in the fewest commu-
nication rounds?
3) Does the algorithm with the best pooled score also
provide the strongest macro-average and per-client
performance?
4) How closely can each federated model approach
centralized training while improving over local-only
models?

III. M ETHODOLOGY AND EXPERIMENTAL SETUP
A. Proposed Methodology
This study proposes a federated learning framework for bi-
nary floodwater segmentation using ETCI 2021, MMFlood, and
Kuro Siwo as three clients. Each client retains its images and
annotations locally, while a coordinating server distributes a
shared U-Net and combines client updates. The objective is
to compare performance across geography, dataset size, image
characteristics, and annotation procedures.
Four algorithms will be compared: FedAvg, FedProx, Fed-
Nova, and SCAFFOLD. FedAvg provides weighted model av-
eraging [2]; FedProx limits local departures through a proximal
penalty [3]; FedNova normalizes updates for unequal local com-
putation [5]; and SCAFFOLD uses control variates to reduce
client drift [4]. Architecture, preprocessing, loss, initial weights,
and evaluation remain common, making the federated algorithm
the principal experimental variable.
The workflow comprises data auditing, label harmonization,
group-level splitting, image preparation, local training, server
aggregation, validation-based selection, and held-out testing.
Raw images and masks remain local during federated training,
but this is not a formal privacy guarantee; differential privacy and
secure aggregation are outside the study. Equal local steps define
the main experiment, and a second condition introduces unequal
work. Three local-only models and one pooled centralized model
assess the benefit of collaboration and the gap to centralized
training. At each communication round, the server broadcasts its
current weights, clients optimize on their own training tiles, and
the selected algorithm forms the next global model. Centralized
pooling is used only as a comparison condition, separate from
the data-local federated procedure.
B. Model Architecture
A standard U-Net [21] receives a 256 ×256 ×2 input, ordered
post-event VV then VH, and produces one floodwater logit per
pixel. Its encoder learns contextual features, while the decoder
and skip connections restore spatial detail for flood extent and
boundaries.
Four encoder blocks have 32, 64, 128, and 256 channels, sepa-
rated by 2 × 2 max pooling. A 512-channel bottleneck connects
to decoder blocks with 256, 128, 64, and 32 channels. Every
block contains two 3 × 3 convolutions, stride 1 and zero padding
1, each followed by group normalization and ReLU. Normal-
ization uses eight groups and ε = 10−5, avoiding reliance on
client-dependent batch statistics. Decoder features are bilinearly
upsampled by two with align_corners=False , concatenated
with matching encoder features, and processed by the block.
Bottleneck dropout is 0.1. A 1 × 1 convolution produces logits;
sigmoid yields probabilities.
Training starts from scratch. A pretrained ResNet50 encoder
is excluded to avoid introducing RGB-to-SAR weight adaptation
as another variable. Within each seed run, all federated and
reference models start from identical saved weights.
C. Dataset and Preprocessing
Dataset sources: Fixed snapshots are blanchon/
ETCI-2021-Flood-Detection , revision 921e207 [22];
links-ads/mmflood , revision 24ca097 [23]; and
orion-ai-lab/Kuro-Siwo-Webdataset , revision
e8e61b7 [24]. Full revisions and checksums will be re-
tained. ETCI uses only data/train samples with matched VV ,
VH, and flood labels; competition test labels were withheld.
MMFlood version 1.0.0 [25] requires matched s1_raw , mask ,
and hydro files. Kuro Siwo uses labeled train_GRD and
test_GRD , including event metadata and valid-pixel masks;
unlabeled and SLC components are excluded. Only post-event
VV/VH enter the model. Elevation, optical imagery, pre-event
SAR, and hydrography are excluded from inputs.
Label harmonization: Output masks use 1 for flood, 0 for valid
non-flood, and 255 for ignore. ETCI raw encoding is checked
first: valid {0,255} masks are decoded to {0,1}, while {0,1}
masks are accepted directly [26]. Ignore value 255 is assigned
only after decoding. Water-body masks are inspected but do
not automatically erase flood positives. Kuro Siwo class 2 maps
to flood, classes 0 and 1 to non-flood, and valid_mask =0 to
ignore [24].
For MMFlood, let F and H denote decoded flood and hy-
drography masks. Valid F = 1,H = 0 pixels are positive; valid
F = 0 pixels are negative; F = 1,H = 1 conflicts are ignored.
Flood-mask value 255, raster no-data, nonfinite SAR values, and
undocumented codes are ignored [ 27]. Hydrography accepts
verified binary {0,1} or {0,255} encoding. Requiring hydrogra-
phy excludes 736 of 1,748 source scenes, leaving at most 1,012
before integrity checks [27]. Actual scene, event, tile, and pixel
counts will be recorded after filtering. Mask values and over-
lays are inspected before training; label rules are fixed before
examining test predictions and applied identically across subsets.
The target represents source-annotated flood extent rather than
uniformly verified temporary inundation; unsupported encod-
ings require resolution before inclusion. All-invalid tiles are
removed. Incomplete hydrography and annotation differences
remain limitations [28].
Data separation: Splitting precedes tiling. ETCI uses
Nebraska for training, North Alabama for validation, and
Bangladesh for testing, keeping all dates within each region
together. This is a regional split, not a 60/20/20 event split. MM-
Flood groups all areas and dates of an EMS activation, then
assigns approximately 60/20/20 of retained groups. Sorted iden-
tifiers are shuffled with NumPy default_rng(42) ; validation
and test each receive max(1, ⌊0.2G⌋) groups, with the remainder
for training.
Kuro Siwo retains its official ten-event test partition and holds
out 20% of remaining groups for validation using the same
procedure. Each subset requires a usable group and valid flood-
positive pixels. If necessary, MMFlood and Kuro development
allocations are retried with seeds 43–141; ETCI permutations
are checked lexicographically. If none is feasible, grouped cross-
validation must be specified instead of splitting related tiles.
Cross-dataset acquisition overlaps are checked; copies overlap-
ping fixed test groups are excluded from training and validation.
One saved split manifest governs all methods.
Image preparation: For ETCI, each RGB polarization PNG
is converted to floating-point intensity I = (R + G + B)/3 be-
fore normalization; VV and VH intensities form the two in-
put channels [26]. Non-overlapping 256 × 256 tiles remain on

vertical flips with probability 0.5 and uniform 0,90,180,270◦
rotations, identically to images and masks. Evaluation has no
random augmentation; valid non-flood tiles remain eligible.

local epochs, which require different numbers of updates for
differently sized datasets. Client contribution weights use eli-

aggregation or correction, with common data ordering where
possible. Validation follows every round. Selection uses the high-

Unequal computation assigns 10, 20, and 30 local steps, rotat-

E. Evaluation Metrics
Flood-class IoU is primary because it penalizes false alarms
and missed inundation. Dice/F1 measures overlap, while pre-
cision and recall distinguish false alarms from missed water.
Reporting both is necessary because recall can increase when
excessive areas are predicted as flooded. These complementary
measures are:
IoU = T PT P+ FP + FN , Dice/F1 = 2T P2T P+ FP + FN ,
(3)
Precision = T PT P+ FP , Recall = T PT P+ FN . (4)
T P, FP, and FN represent correctly detected flood, falsely pre-
dicted flood, and missed flood pixels. Precision assesses false
alarms; recall assesses missed inundation. Accuracy does not se-
lect checkpoints because dominant non-flood areas can conceal
poor detection.
The threshold is fixed at 0.5 and ignored pixels excluded.
Counts accumulate over valid test pixels within each client be-
fore calculating metrics; zero-denominator metrics are undefined.
Results include per-client scores, their equally weighted macro
mean, and pooled scores from combined counts. Means and
standard deviations across seeds, validation-IoU convergence
curves, and selected rounds will be reported. Improvements are
assessed per client and through the macro mean. Generalization
is measured on held-out groups within the three domains; a com-
pletely new dataset is not evaluated. Differences in annotation
quality and the loss of pre-event temporal context will be consid-
ered when comparing client scores. Pooled results are contextual
because larger clients can dominate them, whereas the macro
mean gives each source equal weight.
